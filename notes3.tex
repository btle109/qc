\section{Quantum Simulation}
There are possible advantanges to using quantum computing to solve problems of simulation, largely due to a classical requirement of performing an exponential number of calculations (ex. differential equations).
\begin{tcolorbox}\textbf{Exercise 4.46} Let $\rho$ be a density matrix describing a state of $n$ qubits. Show that describing $\rho$ requires $4^n - 1$ independent real numbers.\\
	Consider $\rho$ with $n$ qubits. Then, $\rho$ is a $2^n \times 2^n$ matrix. Since $\rho$ is Hermitian, we only need $\frac{4^n - 2^n}{2}$ complex numbers to describe off-diagonal elements $\implies 4^n - 2^n$ real numbers. Then, since there are $2^n$ elements on the diagonal, we can use the fact that $\sum_i \rho_ii = 1 \implies $ we need $2^n - 1$ elements to describe the diagonal.
	$$\iff 4^n - 2^n + 2^n -  1 = 4^n - 1$$
\end{tcolorbox}
First we verify the solution to the time-independent Schrodinger equation (quick review of differential equations). Naturally initial condition is that at $t = 0, \ket{\psi(0)}$.
\begin{align*}
	i \frac{d\ket{\psi}}{dt} &= H \ket{\psi}\\
	\frac{1}{\ket{\psi} }d \ket{\psi} &= -iH dt\\
	\intertext{Integrating both sides,}
					  &\iff \text{ln} \ket{\psi} = -iHt + C\\
					  &\iff \ket{\psi(t)} = ce^{-iHt}
	\intertext{By the initial condition,}
					  &\iff \ket{\psi(0)} = ce^0 = c\\
					&\iff \ket{\psi(t)} = e^{-iHt}\ket{\psi(0)}
\end{align*}
A first order linear approximation (ex. $y(t + \delta t) = y(t) + \frac{dy}{dt} \delta t$) is generally insufficient. Instead, we express Hamiltonian as a sum over local interactions.
$$H = \sum^L_{k=1} H_k$$
Each $e^{-iH_kt}$ acts on a smaller subsystem and is easier to approximate, but they must commute to treat them like straightforward sum.
\begin{tcolorbox}\textbf{Exercise 4.47} For $H = \sum_k^L H_k$ prove that $e^{-iHt} = e^{-iH_1t} \dots e^{-iH_lt}$ for all $t$ if $[H_j, H_k] = 0$ for all $j,k$.\\
	Suppose the commutator is zero for all $j,k$. Then, the matrices commute:
	$$e^{-iH_1t}\dots e^{-iH_L t} = e^{-it \sum_k^L H_k} = e^{-iHt}$$
\end{tcolorbox}
The next crucial element is the Trotter formula. The proof is kind of complicated but just recall the binomial theorem and also the Taylor expansion of $e^x$ which are as follows:
$$e^x = \sum_k^\infty \frac{x^k}{k!}, (x+y)^n = \sum^n_{k=0} \binom{n}{k} x_{n-k}y_k$$
The Trotter formula is as follows:
$$\lim_{n\to\infty} (e^{iAt/n} e^{iBt/n})^n = e^{i(A+B)t}$$
Other useful formulas include:
$$e^{i(A+B)\Delta t} = e^{iA \Delta t} e^{i B \Delta t} + O(\Delta t^2)$$
$$e^{i(A+B)\Delta t} = e^{i A \Delta t/2} e^{iB \Delta t}e^{iA \Delta t/2} + O(\Delta t^3)$$

\subsection{Simulation Algorithm}
Consider inputs $H = \sum_k H_k$ acting on $N$ dimensional system, which each $H_K$ acting on a smaller subsystem. An initial state $\ket{\psi_0}, t=0$. An accuracy $\delta > 0$, and $t_f$ (final time).\\
Then, the algorithm proceeds as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Initialize $\ket{\tilde{\psi}}$ of $n = \text{poly}(\log N)$ qubits and operators $e^{-iH_k \Delta t}$. 
	\item Select approximation method (for instance, choosing $U_{\Delta t} = e^{-iH_1 \Delta t/j}\dots e^{-iH_k \Delta t/j} $ using Trotter formula or $U_{\Delta t j} = e^{-iH_j \Delta t}$ using the second approximation.)
	\item Choose $j$ and $\Delta t$ such that depending on approximation method (since some have different errors rates ex. second has $O(\Delta t^2)$, third has $O(\Delta t^3)$. $j, \Delta t$ determine our "step size", since $j \Delta t = t_f$. 

	\item Construct circuit: For $i \in 0\dots j$:
		\begin{enumerate}
			\item $ \ket{\psi_i} \to \ket{\tilde{\psi}_i}$
			\item $\ket{\tilde{\psi}_{i+1}} = U_{\Delta t} \ket{\tilde{\psi}_i}$
			\item $i++;$
		\end{enumerate}
	\item Return $\ket{\tilde{\psi}_i}$
\end{enumerate}
Our output is $\ket{\tilde{\psi}_j} = \ket{\tilde{\psi}(t_f)}$, our estimation of $\ket{\psi(t_f)}$.
Ask Sawyer to explain runtime for simulation algorithm (where does O(poly($\frac{1}{\delta}$)) come from?) (Ans: It comes from the error bound that we are aboutto prove! You can show algebraically fairly easily.)
\begin{tcolorbox}\textbf{Exercise 4.49 (Baker-Campbell-Hausdorf formula)}\\
	Prove that:
	$$e^{(A+B)\Delta t} = e^{A \Delta t} e^{B \Delta t}e^{-\frac12 [A,B] \Delta t^2} + O(\Delta t^3)$$
	and prove the two other useful formulas.
	\begin{proof}
		We prove using Taylor expansions.\\
		\begin{align*} 
			e^{(A+B)\Delta t} &= I + (A+B)\Delta t + \frac{(A+B)^2\Delta t^2}{2} + O(\Delta t^3)\\
				&= I + (A+B)\Delta t + \frac{(A^2 + B^2 + AB + BA)\Delta t^2}{2} + O(\Delta t^3)\\
			e^{A\Delta t}e^{B \Delta t} &= I + (A + B) \Delta t + \frac{(A^2 + B^2 + 2AB)\Delta t^2}{2} + O(\Delta t^3)\\
			e^{A\Delta t}e^{B \Delta t}e^{-\frac12[A,B]\Delta t^2} &=  I + (A + B) \Delta t + \frac{(A^2 + B^2 + 2AB)\Delta t^2}{2} - \frac{(AB-BA)\Delta t^2}{2} +  O(\Delta t^3)\\  
  &= I + (A + B) \Delta t + \frac{(A^2 + B^2 + AB +BA)\Delta t^2}{2} + O(\Delta t^3)
		\end{align*}
		As desired.\\
		We see immediately that the second approximation formula is true since $O(\Delta t^2) = \frac12(A^2 + B^2 + AB +BA)\Delta t^2  + O(\Delta t^3)$.
	\end{proof}
\end{tcolorbox}

\section{The Quantum Fourier Transform}
Consider the discrete Fourier transform, which takes as input a vector of complex numbers, $x_0, \dots, x_{N-1}$ where length $N$ is a fixed parameter, outputing $y_0 \dots y_{N-1}$ defined by:
$$y_k \equiv \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum^{N-1}_{j=0} x_j e^{2\pi ijk/N}$$
The quantum formula transform is identical. Consider orthonormal basis: $\ket0, \dots, \ket{N-1}$. The QFT acts as follows:
$$\ket{j}\to \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum^{N-1}_{k=0} e^{2\pi i jk/ N} \ket{k}.$$
Think of it this way: a quantum state is a wavefunction. The QFT transforms the amplitudes to amplitudes corresponding to the DFT (a func. of frequency instead of time). Thus we have:
$$\ket{\psi} = \sum^{N-1}_{j=0} x_j \ket{j} \to \sum^{N-1}_{k=0} y_k \ket{k}$$
\begin{tcolorbox}\textbf{Exercise 5.1:} Give a direct proof that the QFT is unitary.\\
	We can define a matrix for the quantum Fourier transform:
	$$QFT = \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_j (\sum_{k} e^{2\pi i jk/N} \ket{k})\bra{j}$$
	$$QFT^\dag = \sum_j \ket{j} (\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum^{N-1}_{k=0} e^{-2\pi ijk/N}\bra{k}) $$
	$$\iff (QFT^\dag)QFT = \frac{1}{N} \sum_j \ket{j} (\sum_{k=0} e^{-2\pi ijk/N}\bra{k}) \sum_u (\sum_{v} e^{2\pi i u v/N} \ket{v})\bra{u} $$
	$$=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{u,j} \ket{j}(\sum_{v,k} e^{2\pi i(uv - jk)/N}\braket{k|v})\bra{u}$$
	By orthonormality of $k,v$:
	$$=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{u,j} \ket{l}(\sum_{k} e^{2\pi ik(u - j)/N})\bra{u}$$
	If $u = j$, $$\sum^{N-1}_{k=0} e^{2\pi ik(u - j)/N}  = \sum^{N-1}_{k=0} = N$$
	Observe that $\sum e^{2\pi(u-j)k}$ is a geometric series $ = \frac{1-e^{2 \pi i(u-j)}}{1-e^{2\pi i(u-j)/N}}$. If $u \neq j,$ then $e^{2\pi i k(u-j)}= e^{2 \pi i m}= 1 \implies$ the geometric sum is zero, $m \in \mathbb{Z}$.  
Therefore we see that:
$$=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{u,j} \ket{j}(\sum_{k} e^{2\pi ik(u - k)/N})\bra{u}$$
the middle term is zero when $u\neq j$ and is $N$ when $u = j \iff$ the middle term is equal to $N\delta_{uj}$
$$=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{u,j} \ket{j} N\delta_{uj} \bra{u} = \sum_u \ket{u}\bra{u} = I$$


\end{tcolorbox}
\begin{tcolorbox}\textbf{Exercise 5.2} Compute the Fourier transform of the $n$ qubit state: $\ket{00\dots0}$.\\
	For an $n$ qubit state, $N-1 = 2^n - 1$. 
	$$\ket{00\dots0} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \sum_{k=0} e^{2\pi i (0) k/ N} \ket{k} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\sum_k \ket{k}$$
	$$=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\ \vdots \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$$
\end{tcolorbox}

Generally as we have shown $N=2^n$ where $n$ is the number of qubits and the basis $\ket0 \dots \ket{2^{n-1}}$. We then can write out state $j$ in binary representation: $j = j_1j_2\dots j_n = j_1(2^{n-1} + j_2(2^{n-2}) + \dots + j_n 2^0)$.Similarly, $0.j_l j_{l+1} \dots j_m$ represents binary fraction $j_{l/2} + j_{l+1} + \dots + j_m / 2^{m-l+1}$. We then can define the product representation:
\begin{align*}
	\ket{j} &\to \frac{1}{2^{n/2}} \sum^{2^n-1}_{k=0} e^{2\pi i j k/2^n} \ket{k}\\
		&= \frac{1}{2^{n/2}} \sum^1_{k_1=0}\dots \sum^1_{k_n= 0} e^{2\pi ij \sum^n_l k_l 2^{-l}}\ket{k_1\dots k_n}\\
		&= \frac{1}{2^{n/2}} \sum^1_{k_1=0}\dots \sum^1_{k_n= 0} \bigotimes^n_{l=1} e^{2\pi ij \sum^n_l k_l 2^{-l}}\ket{k}\\
		&= \frac{1}{2^{n/2}} \bigotimes^n_{l=1} [\sum^1_{k_l = 0} e^{2\pi i jk_l 2^{-l}} \ket{k_l}]\\
		&= \frac{1}{2^{n/2}} \bigotimes^n_{l=1} [\ket0 + e^{2\pi i j 2^{-l}}\ket{1}]\\
		&= \fbox{$\frac{1}{2^{n/2}}(\ket0 + e^{2\pi i 0.j_n }\ket1)(\ket0 + e^{2\pi 0.j_{n-1}j_n}\ket1)\dots(\ket0 + e^{2\pi i 0.j_1j_2\dots j_n} \ket1)$}
\end{align*}
Cleanly:
$$\fbox{$\ket{j_1,\dots,j_n} \mapsto \frac{1}{2^{n/2}}(\ket0 + e^{2\pi i 0.j_n }\ket1)(\ket0 + e^{2\pi 0.j_{n-1}j_n}\ket1)\dots(\ket0 + e^{2\pi i 0.j_1j_2\dots j_n} \ket1)$}$$
We now move onto building a circuit for the QFT.\\

Consider gate $R_k$:
$$R_k \equiv \begin{bmatrix} 1&0\\0&e^{2\pi i /2^k}\end{bmatrix}$$
To apply the $QFT$ on $\ket{j_i}$ for $1\leq i \leq n$ apply Hadamard and then controlled $R_j, j $ from $2,n-i+1$ if $2 \leq n-i+1$ gates controlled on the $jth$ qubit. More simply we have this action on one qubit:
$$\ket{j_1 \dots j_n} \to H\ket{j_1} \otimes \ket{j_2\dots j_n} \to (R_2\dots R_n)H \ket{j_1} \otimes \ket{j_2\dots j_n}$$
Tensored with the action on the rest of the qubits ($H$ and $R_k$) we get the QFT.
\begin{tcolorbox}\textbf{QFT Walkthrough}
More explicitly:
For state $\ket{j_1\dots j_n}$
$$H\ket{j_1} \otimes \ket{j_2 \dots j_n} = \frac{1}{2^{1/2}}(\ket0 + e^{2\pi i 0.j_1} \ket1) \ket{j_2\dots j_n}$$
It is clear this is equivalent to std. Hadamard since, if $j_1 = 0$ the sign of $\ket1$ is positive, if $j_1 = 1, e^{\pi i} = -1 \implies $ sign of $\ket1$ is negative. Applying controlled $R_2$ gate (controlled on $j_2$) yields:
$$\frac{1}{2^{1/2}}(\ket0 + e^{2\pi i 0.j_1j_2})\ket{j_2 \dots j_n}$$
since controlled $R_k$ on bit $j_k$ has action of $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\ket0+\ket{1} \to \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\ket0 + e^{2\pi i 0....j_k}\ket1$.
Then, applying the rest of controlled $R_k$ operations yields:
$$= \frac{1}{2^{1/2}}(\ket0 + e^{2\pi i 0.j_1j_2\dots j_n})\ket{j_2 \dots j_n}$$
Then apply Hadamard to the second qubit.
$$= \frac{1}{2^{2/2}}(\ket0 + e^{2\pi i 0.j_1j_2\dots j_n}) (\ket0 + e^{2\pi i 0.j_2} \ket1)\ket{j_3\dots j_n}$$
Apply $R_3 \dots R_n$:
$$= \frac{1}{2^{2/2}}(\ket0 + e^{2\pi i 0.j_1j_2\dots j_n}) (\ket0 + e^{2\pi i 0.j_2j_3\dots j_n} \ket1)\ket{j_3\dots j_n}$$
Repeating to the $n$th qubit yields:
$$= \frac{1}{2^{n/2}}(\ket0 + e^{2\pi i 0.j_1j_2\dots j_n}) (\ket0 + e^{2\pi i 0.j_2j_3\dots j_n} \ket1) \dots (\ket0 + e^{2\pi i 0.j_n} \ket1)  $$
which is the product state definition of QFT as defined above.
\end{tcolorbox}

\section{Phase Estimation}
Phase estimation is an algorithm we can use to estimate the eigenvalues of some unitary operator $U$. We do need a black box/Oracle unfortunately which limits the usefulness. The blackbox should be able to prepare state $\ket{u}$ (eigenvector) and do controlled-$U^{2j}$.\\
We use two registers, the first has $t$  qubits in state $\ket{0}$ (we chooes $t$ based on accuracy and probability of success). The second register has $\ket{u}$ with as many necessary qubits.\\
We apply Hadamard gate onto the first $t$ qubits. Then, on the $k$th qubit, we use that qubit as a control qubit to perform operation $U^{2^{k-1}}$ on $U$. Since this results in $U^m\ket{u} = e^{2\pi i (m \phi)} \ket u$ (which is a phase shift!) applied to the qubits $\ket{u}$, the phenomenon of phase kickback (aka the phase shift "moving up" to the control qubit) changes the $i$th control qubit to:
$$\ket0 \to_H \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\ket0 + \ket1 \to \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\ket0 + e^{2\pi i (2^{k-1}\phi)}\ket1$$
$$\ket{u} \to_{U^{2^{k-1}}} e^{2\pi i (2^{k-1} \phi)}\ket{u}$$
\begin{tcolorbox}\textbf{Exercise 5.7} Show the action of the circuit is to take $\ket{j}\ket{u} \to \ket{j}U^j \ket{u}$.\\
	This is clear based on definition of controlled operation.
\end{tcolorbox}
We can see that the result of the first half of the algorithm on the $t$ qubits is:
$$\frac{1}{2^{t/2}} (\ket{0} + e^{2\pi i 2^{t-1}\phi}\ket{1})\dots(\ket0 + e^{2\pi i 2^0 \phi}\ket1)$$
$$= \frac{1}{2^{t/2}} \sum^{2^t-1}_{k=0} e^{2\pi i \phi k}\ket{k}$$
We can express $\phi = 0.\phi_1 \dots \phi_t$ since the integer component of $\phi$ is irrelevant since $e^{i2\pi k} = e^{i2\pi}$ for $k \in \mathbb{Z}$. Then, the powers of 2 act as bit-shifting and since we can repeatedly ignore the integer components:
$$=\frac{1}{2^{t/2}}(\ket 0 + e^{2\pi i 0.\phi_t}\ket1)\dots(\ket0 + e^{2\pi i 0.\phi_1\dots\phi_t}\ket1)$$
We then perform the inverse quantum Fourier transform before measuring the first register. Applying the inverse quantum Fourier transform as one can see from the definition of product-state QFT yields state:
$\ket{\phi_1 \dots \phi_t}$. A measurement in the computational basis then gives us $\phi$.
The inv. Fourier transform acts as follows:
$$\frac{1}{2^{t/2}} \sum^{2^t-1}_{j=0} e^{2\pi i \phi j} \ket{j} \ket{u}\to \ket{\tilde{\phi}}\ket{u}$$
Our analysis of the performance and requirements yields the following:
Suppose $b$ is a $t$-digit approximation. Suppose $m$ is the final measurement outcome.
Through some tough work we can see that:
$$p(|m-b| > e) \leq \frac{1}{2(e-1)} \implies, e=2^{t-n} -1$$
$$\implies p(|\phi_{error}| < e) \geq 1 - \frac{1}{2(2^p-2)}$$
where $t=n+p = n + [log (2 + \frac{1}{2\epsilon})]$.

\subsection{Phase Estimation Algorithm:}
Consier a blackbox that performs controlled-$U^j$ operations (for $j \in \mathbb{Z}$) and an eigenstate $\ket{u}$ of $U$ with eigenvalue $e^{2\pi i \phi u}$ and $t= n + \text{ceil}(\log(2+\frac{1}{2\epsilon}))$ qubits initialized to zero. The output is an $n$ bit approximation $\tilde{\phi}$ of $\phi$.
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Initialize: $\ket{0}\ket{u}$
	\item Apply Hadamard gates to $n$ qubits: $\xrightarrow[H_j]{} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^t}} \sum_{j=0}^{2^t-1} \ket{j}\ket{u}$
	\item Apply $2^j$ controlled operations on $u$, with $j$th bit as control bit: $\xrightarrow[C^j(U)]{} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^t}} \sum_{j=0}^{2^t-1} \ket{j} U^j \ket{u}$
	\item $= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^t}} \sum^{2^t-1}_{j} e^{2\pi i j \phi_u}\ket{j}\ket{u}$
	\item Perform inverse quantum Fourier transform: $\xrightarrow[QFT^\dag]{} \ket{\tilde{\phi_u}}\ket{u}$
	\item Measure the first register, yielding $\tilde{\phi_u}$
\end{enumerate}
The runtime (clear to see from the image) is $O(t^2)$ and one call to controlled $U^j$, with success probability $1-\epsilon$.
\section{Order-finding}
Consider coprime $x, N, x<N$. The $order$ of $x\mod N$ is the least positive integer $r$ such that
$$x^r \equiv 1(\mod N) \iff x^r - 1 = Nk, k \in \mathbb{Z}$$
\textbf{Bezout's Identity:} For integers a,b: $GCD(a,b) = ax + by, x,y \in \mathbb{Z}$.\\
textbf{Corollary A4.4}: Let $n$ be an integer greater than 1. An integer $a$ has a multiplicative inverse mod $n$ if and only if $gcd(a,n) = 1$, that is, $a$ and $n$ are coprime.
\begin{tcolorbox}\textbf{Exercise 5.10} Show the order of $x=5 \mod N = 21$ is 6.\\
\textbf{Solution:}
$$5^6 - 1 = 21k, k=744$$
Thus we have ver:ified that the congruence holds. It is not difficult to check it is the smallest by verifying that 1,2,3,4,5 are invalid.
\end{tcolorbox}
\begin{tcolorbox}\textbf{Exercise 5.11} Show that the order of $x$ satisfies $r\leq N$.\\
\textbf{Solution:}\\
Consider list $x^0, x^1, \dots x^{N} \mod N$. There are $N$ possible values which are $0, \dots, N-1$. Since it is a list of length $N+1$, it must be that there exists some $n,m$ such that $x^n = x^m \mod N$. Suppose $n>m$. Then, since $x,N$ are coprime there exists a unique inverse for $x$. Take $(x^{-1})^m = x^{-m}$. 
$$x^n \equiv x^m \mod N$$
$$x^n x^{-m} \equiv x^m x^{-m} \mod N$$
$$x^{n-m} \equiv 1 \mod N$$
Choose $r = n-m$. Since $n>m, 0 < r \leq N$. 
\end{tcolorbox}
The quantum algorithm for order-finding is phase estimation algorithm applied to unitary operator (for $0 \leq y \leq N-1$):
	$$U\ket{y} \equiv \ket{xy(\bmod N)}$$
	$$\ket{y} \mapsto \ket{xy (\bmod N)}$$
	$$U = \sum_{y}\ket{xy(\bmod N)}\bra{y}$$
We can see that the eigenvector of $U$ is:
$$\ket{u_s} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \sum^{r-1}_{k=0} e^{\frac{-2\pi i sk}{r}} \ket{x^k\bmod N}$$
$$U \ket{u_s} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \sum^{r-1}_{k=0} e^{-2\pi isk/r} \ket{x^{k+1}\bmod N}$$
Let $j=k+1, k=j-1:$
$$=\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \sum^{r}_{j=1} e^{\frac{-2\pi is (j-1)}{r}} \ket{x^j \bmod N} =\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} e^{2\pi is/r} \sum^{r}_{j=1} e^{\frac{-2\pi is j}{r}} \ket{x^j \bmod N}$$
$$= e^{\frac{-2\pi isj}{r}} \ket{u_s}$$
\begin{tcolorbox}\textbf{Exercise 5.12} Show that $U$ is unitary.\\
	From the definition, we can see that: $$U^\dag = \sum_v \ket{v} \bra{xv(\bmod N)}$$
	$$\implies U^\dag U = \sum_{v,w} \ket{v} \braket{xv(\bmod N) | xw (\bmod N)} \bra{w}$$
	If $xv \equiv xw \mod N \iff \braket{xv(\bmod N) | xw (\bmod N)} = 1$, we see that:
	$$N| xv-xw \iff N| x(v-w)$$
	Since $N,x$ are coprime, it must be that (by corollary A4.4) that $x$ has multiplicative inverse $\mod N$. Multiply both sides by $x^{-1}$. Then,
	$$N|v-w \iff v \equiv w \mod N$$
	Since $v,w < N, v \equiv w \mod N \implies v=w$.\\
	We have shown that if $xv \equiv xw (\mod N) \implies v=w$ for coprime $x,N$ and $v,w < N$.
	It is also trivial to see that $v=w \implies xv \equiv xw (\mod N)$.
	Thus we see that $xv \equiv xw (\mod N) \iff v=w$
	$$\iff \braket{xv (\bmod N) | xw (\bmod N)} = \delta_{vw}$$
	$$U^\dag U = \sum_{v,w} \delta_{vw} \ket{v}\ket{w} = \sum_{v} \ket{v}\bra{v}= I$$
\end{tcolorbox}
Recall for phase estimation we need an oracle to do controlled $U^{2^j}$ operation for any integer $j$ and we must bea ble to prepare eigenstate $\ket{u_s}$ with nontrivial eigenvalue. We do the first with modular exponentiation which we can implement controlled-$U^{2^j}$ using $O(L^3)$ gates.\\
It is difficult to know $\ket{u}$ but we can put them all in uniform superposition which happens to hilariously enough be equal to $\ket{1}$.
$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \sum_{s=0}^{r-1} \ket{u_s} = \ket{1}$$
Then, we get a good estimation of phase $\phi \approx s/r$ at the end of the phase estimation. 
\begin{tcolorbox}\textbf{Exercise 5.13:} Prove (5.44) (Hint: $\sum_{s=0}^{r-1} exp(-2\pi i sk/r) = r \delta_{k0}$). In fact, prove that:
	$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \sum^{r-1}_{s=0} e^{2\pi isk/r} \ket{u_s} = \ket{x^k \bmod N}$$
\textbf{Solution:}\\
First we prove (5.44), showing that the superposition is equal to $\ket{1}$.
$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \sum^{r-1}_{s=0}\ket{u_s} = \frac{1}{r} \sum^{r-1}_{s=0} \sum^{r-1}_{k=0} e^{2\pi isk/r}\ket{x^k \mod N}$$
$$= \frac{1}{r} \sum^{r-1}_{k=0} \sum^{r-1}_{s=0} e^{2\pi isk/r}\ket{x^k \mod N} $$
$$= \sum^{r-1}_{k=0} \delta_{k0} \ket{x^k \mod N}$$
$$= \ket{x^0 \mod N} = \ket{1}$$
Then we show the other equality:
$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \sum^{r-1}_{s=0} e^{2\pi isl/r} \ket{u_s} = \frac{1}{r} \sum^{r-1}_{k=0} \sum^{r-1}_{s=0}e^{-2\pi is(k-l)/r}\ket{x^k \mod N}$$
$$= \sum^{r-1}_{k=0} \delta_{kl} \ket{x^k \mod N}  = \ket{x^k \mod N}$$
\end{tcolorbox}

The next key to our algorithm is the continued fraction expansion:
\textit{Theorem 5.1}: Suppose $\frac{s}{r}$ is a rational number such that:
$$|\frac{s}{r} - \phi| \leq \frac{1}{2r^2}$$
Then $s/r$ is a convergent of the continued fraction for $\phi$ and thus can be computed in $O(L^3)$ operations using continued fractions algorithm.
Since $\phi$ is an approximation of $s/r$ up to $2L+1$ bits, 
$$|\frac{s}{r}-\phi| \leq 2^{-2L-1} \leq \frac{1}{2r^2}$$ since $r \leq N \leq 2^L$. Essentially, the continued fractions algorithm efficiently produces numbers $s', r'$ with no common factor such that $s'/r' = s/r$.  If $x^{r'} \bmod N = 1$ then $r'$ is the order!. It can fail if phase estimation fails (with prob $\epsilon$) or if $s,r$ have a common factor. The continued fractiosn stuff is kind of neat but not that relevant, it can be computed in $O(L^3)$.\\
Overall we get a $O(L^3)$  (remebmer $L = ceil(\log N)$).

\begin{enumerate}
	\item Initialize $\ket{0}\ket{1}$.
	\item Apply Hadamards to each qubit in first register: $\xrightarrow[H^{\otimes j}]{} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^t}} \sum_{j=0}^{2^t-1} \ket{j}\ket{1}$.
	\item Apply $U_{x,n}, \xrightarrow[U_{x,n}]{} \ket{j}\ket{x^j \bmod N}$

	\item Use continued fractions $\to r$.
\end{enumerate}
\section{Factoring, Shor's algorithm}
This is the big one guys. It turns out order-finding is factoring.\\
We use these two theorems to reduce order-finding to factoring:\\
\textit{Theorem 5.2:} Suppose $N$ is an $L$ bit composite number and $x$ is a non-trivial solution to the equation $x^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{N}$ in the range $1\leq x \leq N$, which is that NEITHER $x \equiv 1 \pmod{N}$ nor $x = N-1 \equiv -1 \pmod{N}$. Then, at least one of gcd($x\pm1, N$) is a non-trivial factor of $N$ that can be computed in $O(L^3)$ operations.
\begin{tcolorbox}\textbf{Elaboration:}\\
	Consider $x^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{N} \iff N | (x^2-1) \iff N | (x+1)(x-1)$. We also now that neither $N| x-1$ nor $N|x+1$. 
Then, since $N$ divides the product of $x-1, x+1$ but not them individually it must then be that $N$ has a common factor with one or the other. Since $x-1 < x+1 < N$, the common factor is not $N$, we can use Euclid's algorithm to compute $gcd(x-1, N)$ and $gcd(x+1, N)$ and thus obtain a non-trivial factor of $N$. There will be some more notes on the appendices later.\\
\textbf{Example:}\\
A solution to $x^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{15}$ is $x=1,4,11$. We discard $x=1$. Then, we see that $(4+1)(4-1) \equiv 0 \pmod{15}$ and $(11+1)(11-1) \equiv 0 \pmod{15}$. We check:
$$gcd(5,15) = 5, gcd (3, 15) = 3$$
$$gcd(12,15) = 3, gcd (10,15)= 5$$
The usefulness of this is suppose we find some order $r$ for some number $a$. $a^r \equiv 1 \pmod{N}$. Then, if youchoose $x=a^{r/2}$ we have the equation above. 
\end{tcolorbox}

\subsection{Reduction of factoring to order finding:}

\textbf{Output:} A non-trivial (not $0, N$) factor of $N$.
\textbf{Runtime:} $O((\log N)^3)$ where the log factor comes from bit representation, $\log N \approx L$.
\begin{enumerate}
	\item If $N$ is even, return factor $2$.
	\item Determine whether $N = a^b$ for integers $a \geq 1, b\geq2$ and if so return $a$.
	\item Randomly choose $x$ in range $1$ tho $N-1$. If $gcd(x,N) > 1$ return as factor. 
	\item Use order-finding subroutine to find order $r$ of $x \bmod N$.
\item If $r$ is even and $x^{r/2} \neq -1 \pmod{N}$ then compute $gcd(x^{r/2} -1,N)$ and $gcd(x^{r/2}+1, N)$ and test to see if one is a non-trivial factor returning that factor if so. Else the algorithm fails.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Period-finding}
Suppose $f$ is a periodic function producing a single bit as output such that $f(x+r) = f(x)$ for some unknown $0 < r < 2^L$ where $x,r \in \{0,1,2,\dots\}$.
\textbf{Input:} a quantum black box $U$ which performs unitary $U\ket{x}\ket{y} \to \ket{x} \ket{y \oplus f(x)}$. A state to store the function evaluation, $\ket{0}$ and also $t = O(L+ \log(1/\epsilon))$ qubits initialized $\ket{0}$.\\ 
\textbf{Output:} The smallest integer $r>0$ such that $f(x+r) = f(x)$.\\
\textbf{Runtime:} One call to $U$ abd $O(L^2)$ operations.
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Intialize: $\ket{0} \ket{0}$
	\item $\xrightarrow[H]{} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^t}} \sum_{x=0}^{2^t-1}\ket{x}\ket{0}$
	\item $\xrightarrow[U]{} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^t}} \sum_{x=0}^{2^t-1} \ket{x}\ket{f(x)} \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{r 2^t}} \sum_{l=0}^{r-1} \sum_{x=0}^{2^t-1} e^{2\pi i lx/r} \ket{x} \ket{\hat{f}(l)}$
	\item $\xrightarrow[iQFT]{} \sum_{l=0}^{r-1} \ket{\tilde{l/r}} \ket{\hat{f}(l)}$
	\item Measure to $\tilde{l/r}$.
	\item Apply continued fractions, return $r$.
\end{enumerate}
We will briefly rephrase this in terms of order-finding. Parallels will be made clear when we directly move onto the hidden subgroup problem. \\ 
For order finding our unitary is $U\ket{y} \to \ket{xy \bmod N}$, whereas for more general period finding we have $U\ket{xy} \to \ket{x}\ket{y\oplus f(x)}$.\\
For order finding, we have eigenvector: $\ket{u_s} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \sum e^{-2\pi isk/r} \ket{x^k \bmod N}$.\\
For period finding we have $\ket{\hat{f}(l)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \sum e^{-2\pi ilk/r} \ket{f(x)}$.
In exercise 5.13, we showed that $\ket{x^k \bmod N} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \sum e^{2\pi isk/r} \ket{u_s}.$\\
Equivalently, for period finding: $\ket{f(x)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \sum^{r-1}_{x=0} e^{2\pi ilx/r} \ket{\hat{f}(l)}.$

		$$\sum_{k \in \{0,r,2r, \dots, N-r\}} e^{2\pi ikl/N} = \sqrt{N/r} \delta_{l, kN/r}$$

		We can express any $x$ as $k+j$, where $k \in \{0,r,2r,\dots, N-r\}$ and $j \in [0, r)$.\\
		Then,
		$$\hat{f}(l) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{k \in K} \sum_{j=0}^{r-1} e^{-2\pi il(k+r)/N} f(x)$$

Using the hint:	
$$= \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \frac{\sqrt{N}}{\sqrt{r}} \sum_{j=0}^{r-1}e^{-2\pi ilr/N} f(x) $$
$$= \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \sum_{j=0}^{r-1}e^{-2\pi ilr/N} f(x) $$
as desired.
\end{tcolorbox}

The natural evolution of the period finding problem which deals with one integer is the discrete logarithm problem. Consider function $f(x_1,x_2) = a^{sx_1 + x_2} \bmod N$ where all variables are integers and $r$ is the order of $a \bmod N  \equiv a^r \bmod N = 1$. This function is periodic with 2-tuple period $(l, -ls)$. This allows us to solve the discrete log problem: which is determining $s$ for $b=a^s$ given some $a,b$. We use unitary $U \ket{x_1}\ket{x_2} \ket{y} \to \ket{x_1} \ket{x_2} \ket{y \oplus f(x)}$ where $\oplus$ is XOR. Assume we already know order from previous order-finding algorithm.\\
\textbf{Input:} A black-box performing the unitary described above with $f(x_1,x_2) = b^{x_1} a^{x_2}$, a state to store the function evaluation initialized to $\ket{0}$ and two $t$ qubit registers initialized to $\ket{0..0}$.
\textbf{Output:} Smallest $s$ such that $a^s = b$. \\
\textbf{Runtime:} One call of $U$ and $O(ceil(\log r)^2)$ operations with $O(1)$ probability.
\begin{enumerate}
	\item $\ket{0}\ket{0}\ket{0}$
	\item $\xrightarrow[H]{} \frac{1}{2^t} \sum_{x_1} \sum_{x_2} \ket{x_1} \ket{x_2} \ket0 $
	\item $\approx \frac{1}{2^t \sqrt{r}} \sum_{l_2} \sum{x_1}\sum_{x_2} e^{2\pi i (s l_2 x_1 + l_2 x_2)/r} \ket{x_1} \ket{x_2} \ket{\hat{f}(s l_2, l_2)}$
	\item $= \frac{1}{2^t \sqrt{r}} \sum_{l_2} [\sum_{x_1} e^{2\pi i (s l_2 x_1)/r} \ket{x_1}] [\sum_{x_2} e^{2\pi i l_2 x_2/r} \ket{x_2}] \ket{\hat{f}(sl_2, l_2)}$
	\item $\xrightarrow[iQFT]{} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \sum_{l_2} \ket{\hat{sl_2/r}} \ket{\hat{l_2/r}} \ket{\hat{f}(sl_2, l_2)}$
	\item Measure the first two registers, yielding: $\hat{s l_2/r}, \hat{l_2/r}$
	\item Apply continued fractions
\end{enumerate}
Functionally it is extremely similar to the previously analyzed period-finding and order finding algorithms. A huge chart will come soon making all the parallels much clearer. Two important steps we will show in the next two exercises.

\subsection{The Hidden Subgroup Problem}
The hidden subgroup problem is as follows:
\begin{tcolorbox}\textbf{Hidden Subgroup Problem:}
	Let $f$ be a function from a finitely generated group $G$ to a finite set $X$ such that $f$ is constant on the cosets of a subgroup $K$ and distinct on each coset. Given a quantum black box for performing the unitary transform $U \ket{g} \ket{h} = \ket{g} \ket{h \oplus f(g)},$ for $g \in G, h \in X$ and $\oplus$ an appropriately chosen binary operation on $X$ find a generating set for $K$.\\

\end{tcolorbox}
\textbf{Definition:}
A [left] coset of a subgoup $K$ is the set composed of every $gk, g \in G, k \in K$. For a group like addition mod $N$, we denote cosets as $1 + K , 2 + K, ...$.\\
\textbf{Definition:} Fourier transform on a Group:\\
You can express a function that maps group to complex numbers as a vector so it basically your function is just like indexing a vector. Then, for some Abelian group $G$ defined by $g \in [0, N-1]$ with addition, defining $\rho_h(g) = e^{-2\pi igh/N}$ as $h$ representation of $g$ (which has one dimensional representation), we can see that the Fourier transform on $G$ are:
$$\hat{f}(h) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{g=0}^{N-1} f(g) e^{-2\pi i gh/N}$$
$$f(h) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{g=0}^{N-1} \hat{f}(g) e^{2\pi i h g/ N}$$
\begin{tcolorbox}\textbf{Exercise A2.23} Show the Fourier transform relations for $G$ are what is stated above. \\
	For $G$ a finite group of order $N$ and $f$ a function that maps group elements to complex numbers, for an irreducible representation $\rho$ of $G$ of dimension $d_\rho$ the Fourier transform is defined as:
	$$\hat{f}(p) \equiv \sqrt{\frac{d_\rho}{N}} \sum_{g \in G} f(g) \rho(g)$$
	For $\rho_p = 1 \rho_h(g) = e^{-2\pi i gh/N}$, we can immediately see that:
	$$\hat{f}(\rho)  = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{g = 0}^{N-1} f(g) e^{-2\pi igh/N}$$
	as desired. We then show the inverse $f(h)$ is defined as so. Substiting the expression for $f(h)$ into $\hat{f}(h)$ yields:
	$$\hat{f}(h) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{g=0}^{N-1} \sum^{N-1}_{k=0} \hat{f}(k) e^{-2\pi i g(k-h)/N} $$
	From exercise 5.13, we know that $\sum_{g}e^{-2\pi i g(k-h)/N} = N\delta_{kh}$ (easily proved with geometric series).
	$$\hat{f}(h) =  \delta_{kh} \sum^{N-1}_{k=0} \hat{f}(k)  = \hat{f}(h)$$

\end{tcolorbox}
The algorithm for Hidden Subgroup is very similar to what we have seen previously.
The algorithm first initializes $\ket{0}\ket{0}$. Then, we use a Fourier transform (generalizing the Hadamard operation) to put the first register into a superposition.\\
We see that $\ket{0}$ is equivalent to a function $\Lambda$ that maps $g=0$ to 1 and any other element in $g$ to $0$. Using the formulation of the QFT as an action on a function from groups to complex numbers, we can define the new state as $\hat{\Lambda}(h) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{g \in G} \Lambda(g) e^{2\pi i g 0/ N} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}$. This function returns $\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}$ for every element in $G$, which is the vector $\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\ket{1...1}$ from our usual understanding (see exercise 5.2). 
$$\ket{0}\ket{0} \xrightarrow[QFT]{}  \frac{1}{\sqrt{G}} \sum_{g \in G}\ket{g} \ket{0} $$
Perform the controlled unitary on the second register (with the first as control):
$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{G}}\sum_{g \in G} U\ket{g} \ket{0} =  \frac{1}{\sqrt{G}}\sum_{g \in G}\ket{g} \ket{f(g)} $$
We rewrite $\ket{f(g)}$ in the Fourier basis:
$$\ket{f(g)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}} \sum_{l=0}^{|G|-1} e^{2\pi i lg/|G|} \ket{\hat{f}(l)}$$
$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}\sum_{g\in G} \ket{g}\ket{f(g)} \approx \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G}\sum_{l \in G} e^{2 \pi i lg/ |G|}\ket{g} \ket{\hat{f}(l)}$$
Since $f$ is constant and distinct on cosets of the subgroup $K$, $\ket{\hat{f}(l)}$ has nearly (?) zero amplitude for all $l$ except for those which satisfy $\sum_{h \in K} e^{-2\pi i lh/ |G|} = |K|$ (equivalently that $l,h$ are orthogonal). Intuitively this is an extension of the usual geometric summation identity we have been using in each other HSP-derived problem.\\
Consider as an example subgroup $K$ with cosets $1+K, \dots, v + K$ where there are $v$ cosets. Then, we can see that any $g \in G$ can be expressed as $g = u + K$, $u \in \{ 1, \dots, v\}, h \in K$. Thus: $\sum_{g \in G} g = \sum_{u}\sum_{h \in K} u + h$.
$$  =\frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G}\sum_{u} \sum_{h \in K} e^{2 \pi i g (u+h)/ |G|}\ket{g} \ket{\hat{f}(u+h)} = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G}\sum_{u} \sum_{h \in K} e^{2 \pi i g u/ |G|} e^{2 \pi i g h/ |G|}\ket{g} \ket{\hat{f}(u+h)}$$
Since $f$ is constant on cosets, $f(u+h) = f(u)$
$$= \frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g \in G} \sum_{u} \sum_{h\in k} e^{2\pi i gh/|G| }e^{2\pi i gu/|G|} \ket{g}\ket{\hat{f}(u)}$$
Using $\sum_{h\in K} e^{2\pi i gh/ |G|} = |K|$ and 0 if $h \not \in K^\perp, \equiv \frac{l \cdot h}{|G|} \in \mathbb{Z}$
$$\approx \frac{|K|}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} \sum_{l \in K^\perp} e^{2\pi igl/ |G|} \ket{g}\ket{\hat{f}(l)}$$
If $l, |G|$ have common factors however then extracting $l$ immediately from the equation may not actually provide useful results. With no guarantee on $l, |G|$ being coprime or anything else like that, we must use the fundamental theorem of finite Abelian groups.\\
\textbf{Fundamental Theorem of Finite Abelian Groups:}\\
Any finite Abelian group $G$ is isomorphic to a product of cyclic groups of prime power order, that is, $G = \mathbb{Z}_{p_1} \times \mathbb{Z}_{p_2} \times \dots \times \mathbb{Z}_{p_m}$ where $p_i$ are primes and $\mathbb{Z}_{p_i}$ is the group over integers mod $p_i$. Therefore the phase equivalently is:
$$e^{2\pi i lg/ |G|} = \prod_{i=1}^M e^{2\pi i l'_i g_i / p_i}$$
Therefore we then have:
$$ = \frac{|K|}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} \sum_{l \in K^\perp} \prod_{i=1}^M e^{2\pi ig_i l'_i/ p_i} \ket{g}\ket{\hat{f}(l)}$$

		\item Solve $ \frac{l \cdot g}{|G|} = 0 \bmod 1  \iff \sum_i \frac{l_i \cdot g_i}{p_i} = 0 \bmod 1$. 
		\item The set of $g$ is the generators of the hidden subgroup.
	 \end{enumerate}

\section{Post-script:}
This is not the standard presentation of period-finding and consequently Hidden Subgroup Problem. The main Fourier-basis re-expression doesn't really work for when $r$ is not a factor of $N$, it becomes incomplete. We redo period-finding and then redo HSP. See Ch.6 Preskill's notes.\\
\textbf{Period-finding (Standard Approach):}\\
	\textbf{Input:} $f$ with period $r$ for some unknown $0 < r < 2^L$ where $x,r \in \{ 0,1,2, \dots\}$. A black box $U$ which performs $U\ket{x}\ket{y} \to \ket{x} \ket{y \oplus f(x)}$. Two registers initialized to zero, $t = O(L + \log(1/e))$ bits for the first one.\\
	\textbf{Output:} Smallest $r>0$ such that $f(x+r) = f(x)$.\\
	\textbf{Runtime:} $O(U) + O(L^2)$ with probability $O(1)$.
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Initialize $\ket{0}\ket{0}$.
	\item Put into even superposition via QFT/Hadamard:
		$$ \xrightarrow[QFT]{} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^t}} \sum_{x=0}^{2^t-1}\ket{x}\ket{0}$$
	\item Apply $U$: 
		$$\xrightarrow[U]{} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^t}} \sum_{x=0}^{2^t-1} \ket{x}\ket{f(x)}$$
\item We then measure the second register. We obtain some $x$, $f(x)$ which then by periodicity is $f(x_0 + kr) = f(x_0) 0 \leq x_0 < r$. Also, define $A$ such that $A - 1 < \frac{N}{r} < A+1$.
		$$\xrightarrow[M]{} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^t}}\sum_{k = 0}^{A-1}\ket{x_0 + kr} \ket{f(x_0 + kr)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^t}} \sum_{k=0}^{A_1} \ket{x_0 + kr}\ket{f(x_0)}$$
	Discarding the second register,
	$$ = \frac{1}{\sqrt{A}}\sum_{k=0}^{A-1}\ket{x_0 + kr}$$
	\item Applying quantum Fourier transform to the first register yields:
		$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{AN}} \sum_{k=0}^{A-1}\sum_{y=0}^{N-1} e^{2\pi i(x_0 + kr)y/N} \ket{y}$$
		$$=\frac{1}{\sqrt{AN}} \sum_{y=0}^{N-1} e^{2\pi i x_0 y/ N}\sum_{k=0}^{A-1} e^{2\pi i kr y/ N} \ket{y}$$
		We use the fact that $\sum_{k=0}^{A-1} e^{2\pi i kry/N} = N/r = A$ (equal to $\sqrt{N/r} =\sqrt{A}$ up to normalization) when $y= sN/r, s\in \mathbb{Z}$ and zero otherwise. Therefore this is:
		$$=\frac{\sqrt{A}}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{y \equiv 0 \bmod N/r} e^{2\pi i x_0 y/N}\ket{y}$$
			You can interpret this as probability being highest where $y\equiv 0 \bmod N/r$ and roughly 0 elsewhere.
		\item Measure to get $y$ with high probability. Then, $y= sN/r \implies y/N \approx s/r$. Use continued fractions to get $r$ from $s/r$.
\end{enumerate}
\section*{Hidden Subgroup Algorithm}
\textbf{Input:}$f$ be a function from a finitely generated group $G$ to a finite set $X$ such that $f$ is constant on cosets of subgroup $K$ and distinct on each coset. A blackbox for $U\ket{g}\ket{h} = \ket{g}\ket{h \oplus f(g)}$ ($\oplus$ an appropriate bin. op.)\\
\textbf{Output:} A generating set for $K$.\\
\textbf{Runtime:} $O(L^2) + O(U)$.
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Begin in state $\ket{0}\ket{0}$ 
	\item Perform QFT on first register and $U$ on second register:
		$$\xrightarrow[QFT,U]{} \frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}\sum_{g \in G} \ket{g} \ket{f(g)}$$
	\item Measure (and discard second register)
		$$\xrightarrow[M]{} \frac{1}{\sqrt{|K|}} \sum_{h \in K} \ket{h + x_0} \ket{f(h+x_0)}$$
		$$\xrightarrow[]{} \frac{1}{\sqrt{|K|}}\sum_{h \in K} \ket{h + x_0}$$
		Note: Since $f$ is constant on cosets, $\ket{f(h+x_0)} = \ket{f(x_0)}$
	\item By Fund. Theorem of finite Abelian groups, $G\cong Z_{N_1} \times \dots \times Z_{N_m}$ and thus we treat $g,h$ as vectors in such a set. Calculate this decomposition using order-finding algorithm. Then, perform QFT on first state:
		$$\xrightarrow[QFT]{} \frac{1}{\sqrt{|G||K|}} \sum_{h \in K} \sum_{g \in G} e^{2\pi i (h+x_0)g/|G|} \ket{g} $$
		$$=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G||K|}} \sum_{g \in G} e^{2\pi i x_0 g/ |G|}\sum_{h \in K} e^{2\pi i h g/ |G|} \ket{g}$$
		By fund.thm:
		$$=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G||K|}} \sum_{g\in G}e^{2\pi i x_0 g/|G|} \sum_{h \in K} e^{2\pi i \sum_j h_j g_j/ N_j} \ket{g_1\dots g_m}$$
			where $N_i = p_i^k$ for some prime $p_i$, integer $k$.
			If $h\cdot g/|G| \equiv \sum_j h_j \cdot g_j / N_j = 0 \bmod {1}$, then $ e^{2\pi i \sum_j h_j g_j/ N_j} = 1$, else it is nearly zero. Thus:
			%$$\approx \frac{\sqrt{|K|}}{\sqrt{|G|}} \sum_{g \in G} e^{2\pi i x_0 g/|G|} \sum_{h \in K^\perp} e^{2\pi i \sum_j h_jg_j/N_j} \ket{g_1 \dots g_m}$$
			$$\approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{|K^\perp|}} \sum_{g \in K^\perp} e^{2\pi i x_0 g/|G|} \ket{g_1 \dots g_m}$$
	\item Measure the second register at most $m$ times, obtaining $m$ instances of $g= (g_1, \dots, g_m)$ such that $\frac{g_1 \cdot h_1}{N_1} + \dots + \frac{g_m \cdot h_m}{N_m}  = 0 \bmod {1} \iff g_1 \cdot h_1 + g_2 \cdot h_2 + \dots = 0 \bmod LCM(N_j) $ for all $h \in H$. We need $m$ samples since at this point it is really solving a system of lin. eq., if we do not have enough samples for $m$ dimensional problem then we will have free variables. Thus we can determine what the generator of $K$ is. 
\end{enumerate}
